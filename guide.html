How to use the GE2030 Simulator

This simulator lets you play "election designer" for Singapore. You redraw the voting map, decide which parties stand where, choose how people's votes change, and then watch a pretend election night unfold, seat by seat.

You don't need to know anything about politics to use it. This guide explains every button and every election word in plain English, with pictures. It takes about 10 minutes to read, and you can jump to any part using the contents list.

Just want to play? Open the simulator, click the 4 Election night tab and press ▶ Polls close — start the count. You'll see the 2025 map play out as a live results night. Come back here when you want to understand what's going on.

Singapore elections in 2 minutes

Every few years, Singapore holds a General Election (GE) to choose the Members of Parliament (MPs) who make the country's laws. The most recent one was GE2025, held on 3 May 2025. The next one is often called GE2030, because it is due by around then. That is the election this simulator imagines.

Singapore is not one big vote. The island is cut into areas called constituencies. Each constituency elects its own MP or team of MPs. Whichever party wins the most seats in Parliament forms the government. In GE2025 there were 33 constituencies electing 97 MPs.

There are two kinds of constituency:

  • SMC (Single Member Constituency): a smaller area that elects one MP. Think of it as one person representing one neighbourhood.
  • GRC (Group Representation Constituency): a bigger area that elects a team of 3 to 6 MPs together. Voters choose between whole teams, not individuals. By law, each team must include at least one MP from a minority community (Malay, Indian or another minority), so that Parliament reflects Singapore's mix of races.

Before each election, a government committee called the Electoral Boundaries Review Committee (EBRC) redraws the lines between constituencies, because people move and new towns get built. That's the job you get to do in Step 1.

Glossary of terms

You'll see these words in the simulator. Each has a short definition and an everyday comparison.

Elector
A person allowed to vote: a Singapore citizen aged 21 or older who is on the register of voters. Voting is compulsory in Singapore, so most electors do vote. About 2.76 million people could vote in GE2025.
Constituency (also "electoral division")
One voting area on the map. Like one class in a school vote: each class picks its own representative.
Seat
One place in Parliament, filled by one MP. A 5-member GRC is worth 5 seats.
Electoral boundary
The line on the map that separates one constituency from another. Moving it changes who votes where.
Electors per MP / quota
How many voters each MP represents. For fairness, every MP should represent roughly the same number of people. The "quota" is the national average. The EBRC keeps every constituency within ±30% of it. If one MP speaks for 20,000 people and another for 40,000, the first group's votes count for more.
Contiguous
All in one connected piece. A constituency shouldn't be split into separate islands of houses on the map.
Contest / straight fight / three-cornered fight
A contest is when more than one party stands in a constituency. A straight fight has two parties; a three-cornered fight has three. More parties usually means the opposition vote gets split.
Walkover
When only one party turns up to stand in a constituency, so it wins without any voting. Like winning a match because the other team didn't show up. In GE2025, Marine Parade–Braddell Heights GRC was a walkover.
Vote share
The percentage of valid votes a party gets. Winning 60% means 6 out of every 10 voters picked that party.
Popular vote
A party's vote share added up across the whole country.
Majority
More than half of all seats in Parliament. The party with a majority forms the government. With 97 seats, a majority is 49.
Two-thirds (⅔) supermajority
Two out of every three seats. A party with this many seats can change most parts of the Constitution on its own.
Swing
How much a party's vote share goes up or down compared with last time. It is measured in percentage points ("pp"). If PAP got 60% last time and 55% this time, that's a 5-point swing away from PAP.
Turnout
The percentage of electors who actually voted. It is usually about 92–94% in Singapore because voting is compulsory.
Notional result
A "what-if" result. If you draw a new constituency, it didn't exist in 2025, so the simulator works out how the people inside it voted in 2025. That estimate is the notional result.
Sample count
An early estimate released on election night, before the full count is finished. Officials count a random sample of ballots from each polling station. It is usually accurate to within about 4 percentage points.
Recount
If the winner beats the runner-up by 2% or less, the candidates can ask for the votes to be counted again.
NCMP (Non-Constituency MP)
Singapore guarantees at least 12 opposition voices in Parliament. If fewer than 12 opposition MPs win seats, the best-performing losing opposition candidates are offered extra seats as NCMPs, provided they won at least 15% of the vote in their constituency.
Incumbent / anchor minister
The MP who currently holds the seat. An "anchor minister" is a senior government minister leading a GRC team; they often attract extra votes.
Opposition
Any party that isn't in government. In Singapore that means every party except the PAP.

A tour of the screen

When you open the simulator, you'll see the real GE2025 map. Each coloured patch is one constituency.

Pale areas on the mapVery light patches, like Jurong Island, the airport and the offshore islands, are places where nobody lives. They still belong to a constituency but have no voters.
Step 1

Draw boundaries

In this step you act as the EBRC. You decide which neighbourhoods are grouped together, how many constituencies there are, and whether each one is an SMC or a GRC. If you'd rather keep the real 2025 map, skip to Step 2.

The map starts as the GE2025 boundaries. To start from nothing, click Blank map at the top of the side panel. If you change your mind, GE2025 boundaries brings the real map back.

You can draw by hand with the tools below, or let the computer do it: ✨ Auto-draw redraws the whole island in a few seconds using one of four methods.

Look around: click a constituency

Hover your mouse over any area to see a small information card. Click it to select its constituency. The selected constituency gets a yellow outline and its details appear in the side panel.

The drawing tools

The Tools box in the side panel has six tools. Click one to choose it, or press its keyboard key.

➚ Select VClick an area to select its constituency. You can't change anything with this tool, so it's safe for exploring.
🖌 Paint BClick or drag over areas to move them into the selected constituency.
🪣 Fill GClick once to move a whole connected group of same-coloured areas into the selected constituency. Handy for merging two constituencies.
➰ Lasso LHold the mouse button and draw a loop. Every area inside the loop joins the selected constituency.
⌫ Erase EClick or drag to take areas out of their constituency, leaving them unassigned.
✋ Pan HDrag to move the map around. With any other tool, hold Space and drag to move the map.
Made a mistake?Press the ↶ undo button (or Ctrl+Z) as many times as you like. ↷ (Ctrl+Y) redoes.

Worked example: create a new SMC

Say you think western Sengkang should be its own single-MP constituency. Here's how:

  1. Scroll down the side panel to the constituency list and click + SMC. A new, empty constituency called "New SMC 1" is created and selected, and the Paint tool turns on automatically.
  2. Zoom into Sengkang and click (or drag) over the areas you want in the new seat. They turn the new constituency's colour straight away.
  3. Rename it: click the name box and type "Sengkang West". You can also click the blue "Rename to …" suggestion, which uses the main neighbourhood names.
  4. Check the vs quota number. Aim for green, somewhere between about −15% and +15% for a good, fair seat.

Using the lasso for big areas

Painting block by block is slow for large areas. Use the Lasso instead. Select the constituency you want to add to (or create a new one with + GRC), choose the lasso, then hold down the mouse button and draw a loop around the areas. Let go, and everything inside the loop joins the constituency.

Fixing problems: the Checks box

The Checks box makes sure your map follows the rules. ✖ Errors break a rule. ▲ Warnings are allowed but worth a look. Click any message to jump to the constituency it's about.

The rules checked are:

  • Every populated area must belong to a constituency.
  • At least 8 SMCs, as required by Singapore's Constitution.
  • GRCs have 3 to 6 MPs.
  • Electors per MP within ±30% of the national average, the rule the EBRC follows. You can make this stricter in the Rules box at the bottom of the panel.
  • Each constituency is in one piece. This is a warning only; uninhabited islands don't count.
You don't have to fix everythingThe simulator still runs an election on a map with errors. Fixing them just makes your map more realistic.

Map lenses: see who lives where

The Map lens buttons recolour the map to show facts about each neighbourhood. They help you draw smarter boundaries. For example, you can see where young voters live, or which areas voted most strongly for the opposition in 2025.

2025 or 2030 voters?The 2025 register / 2030 projection switch at the top changes the voter numbers everywhere. The 2030 projection adds about 115,000 extra voters, mostly in estates still filling up, such as Tengah. A seat that is a fair size today may be too big by 2030.

✨ Auto-draw: let the computer draw the map

Drawing about 30 constituencies by hand takes time. Auto-draw does it for you. Click ✨ Auto-draw the whole map under Start from, pick a method, adjust a few settings, and press ✨ Draw the map. A few seconds later you have a complete new map that follows the rules. You can then fine-tune it by hand, or press Undo.

The four methods

🏛️ EBRC-style (realistic)
Works like the real Electoral Boundaries Review Committee. It keeps the existing constituencies and makes small changes, moving a few neighbourhoods between neighbours to fix seats that have too many or too few voters. It can also add seats or SMCs as the population grows. Best for: "What will the actual GE2030 map probably look like?" Try it with the 2030 projection switched on.
📐 Fair & compact
Throws away the old lines and draws a brand-new map from scratch. Every seat has nearly the same number of voters per MP, shapes are neat and round, and towns are split as little as possible. Best for: "What would a neutral, tidy map look like?"
🧩 Custom mix
You decide the exact line-up: how many SMCs, and how many GRCs of 3, 4, 5 or 6 MPs. The All SMCs (97) button gives every voter their own single MP, with no GRCs at all. Best for: "What if Singapore scrapped GRCs?"
🎭 Gerrymander (for learning)
Draws lines on purpose to help one side, using how each neighbourhood voted in 2025. You can favour the PAP, favour the opposition, or create as many close 50-50 seats as possible. Every map it draws still obeys the ±30% rule. Best for: understanding why who draws the lines matters.

What is gerrymandering?

Imagine 3 seats of 10 voters each: 12 people like Party A and 18 like Party B. Put 10 of B's voters together in one seat, and split the rest so the other two seats each have 6 A voters and 4 B voters. Party A now wins 2 of the 3 seats, even though more people voted for B. Gerrymandering uses two tricks:

  • Packing: squeezing the other side's voters into a few seats they win by huge margins, wasting their extra votes.
  • Cracking: splitting the other side's voters across many seats so they narrowly lose everywhere.
Tips
  • Switch the electors to 2030 projection before an EBRC-style redraw. It suggests adding seats (about 101) because of population growth, then rebalances the map.
  • Don't like the result? Press 🎲 Try another variation. Each variation is a different, equally valid map.
  • Auto-draw follows neighbourhood lines, so you can still adjust any seat afterwards with the Paint tool.

Step 2

Contests: who stands where

Now decide which parties put up candidates in each constituency. You don't have to start from scratch: the simulator fills in sensible defaults. Each seat gets the PAP against the opposition party that fought for most of those voters in 2025. Unchanged 2025 constituencies keep their exact 2025 line-up.

Changing a line-up

  1. Click a constituency in the list (or on the map) to open it.
  2. Click party badges to add them (the badge fills in) or remove them (the badge goes hollow). Add a third party to create a three-cornered fight. Leave only one party to create a walkover.
  3. Choose each party's anchor leader: the person who leads the team in a GRC, or the candidate in an SMC (see below).

Anchor leaders

Every party team has a leader. In a GRC it's the team's anchor, often a minister or a party chief; in an SMC it's simply the candidate. For each party in an opened seat:

  • Pick a real person: click the box to choose from that party's actual GE2025 candidates. Well-known figures, such as the Prime Minister, ministers and party leaders, are listed first with their role.
  • Or type any name. This is handy for smaller parties, new faces, or a party you created.
  • Leader effect: the simulator suggests how many extra points a leader brings compared with the 2025 anchor for those voters. For example, the Prime Minister is worth about +3 points, a minister +1.5 and a party leader +1 to +2. Moving a big name to a new seat helps there, and the seat they leave behind loses that boost. The slider is only a suggestion; set it to whatever you believe.

Leaders appear throughout the night: "WP team led by … GAIN from PAP", in the results list, and when you hover over the map.

Party strength and new parties

At the bottom of the Contests panel, the Party strength sliders say how strong each opposition party is. It is set from how well each party did in 2025, with WP at 1.0. A stronger party gets more votes when it stands somewhere new, and takes more of the opposition vote in multi-party fights. You can also add your own party: give it a short code (like "NEW"), a name and a colour, then add it to seats like any other party.

Step 3

Swings & forecast: how will people vote?

This is where you make your prediction. The starting point is always how people voted in 2025. You then say how things will change. The forecast at the top updates instantly as you move any slider.

The five kinds of swing, explained

All swings are in percentage points (pp). Positive numbers move votes towards the PAP; negative numbers move votes towards the opposition. Double-click any slider to reset it to zero.

1. National swing
Every seat moves by the same amount. −5 means the PAP loses 5 points everywhere, e.g. 60% → 55%. For reference, the PAP's national vote went from 61.2% in 2020 to 65.6% in 2025, a swing of about +4.3 towards the PAP.
2. Party swing
One opposition party does better or worse wherever it stands. "WP +3" gives the WP 3 extra points in every seat it contests, taken from the PAP.
3. Demographic swing
A group of voters changes its mind, such as young voters, Malay voters or people in 4-room HDB flats. Each seat is affected according to how many of those voters live there. "Young voters −6" hurts the PAP most in estates full of young families.
4. Constituency swing
A change in one seat only, e.g. because of a local issue or a star candidate. Select the seat on the map or in the table, then use the slider in the Constituency swing box.
5. Turnout change
More or fewer people voting. It has only a small effect on who wins.

Below the sliders, the Seats, closest first table lists every constituency, starting with the most marginal. Seats won by less than 5 points are highlighted. These are the ones to watch on election night.

2,000 simulations: how sure is the forecast?

Real elections have surprises: polls are off and individual seats behave unexpectedly. Click 🎲 2,000 simulations and the simulator reruns your election 2,000 times, each with random surprises. Then it tells you how often each result happened.

The size of the surprises is set in the Uncertainty box near the bottom. National polling error is how far off your whole prediction could be. Seat-level noise is how much individual seats can surprise. After running the simulations, a Prob column appears in the table showing each seat's chance of going to the projected winner.

Step 4

Election night

The fun part. Polls close at 8pm and you watch the results come in, just like on TV.

What happens during the night

  1. ~10:30pm – sample counts. Early estimates arrive for each constituency and the map shows a faint tint of the leading party. They're usually right, but can be a few points off.
  2. The parliament chart fills up. Every seat is a dot. Empty rings are seats still being counted, and each dot pops into a party's colour as its result is declared.
  3. ~11:30pm onwards – official results. Each constituency is declared one by one. Small SMCs come first and big GRCs come in the early morning. The map fills in with the winner's colour.
  4. The call. As soon as a party passes the majority line, a banner announces it.
  5. The end. A final summary shows seats, vote shares, which seats changed hands, and any NCMP seats offered.
Hover for detailsMove your mouse over any declared constituency to see its full result. Press ↻ New election night at the bottom of the panel to replay with a fresh set of surprises.

Saving & sharing your prediction

  • Autosave: your map, contests and swings are saved automatically in your browser. Close the tab and they'll still be there next time, on the same computer and browser.
  • 🔗 Share link: opens a share box. It copies a web link containing your whole prediction, together with a short note saying it was made by an individual for fun and is not a poll, survey or official projection. The note is always included. Anyone who opens the link sees exactly your map, line-ups and swings, with a reminder banner at the top.
  • ⬇ Export: downloads your prediction as a small file (ge2030-map.json). Keep it as a backup, or to try different versions.
  • ⬆ Import: loads an exported file back in.

Tips & FAQ

Is this a real prediction of GE2030?

No. It's a tool for exploring "what if" scenarios, for fun and learning. The results only reflect the choices you make. It isn't affiliated with the Elections Department or any political party.

Where do the numbers come from?

Official public data. Boundaries, 2025 results and voter numbers come from the Elections Department (ELD). Neighbourhood boundaries and land use come from the Urban Redevelopment Authority (URA). Age, ethnicity and housing come from the 2020 Census by the Department of Statistics. All are published on data.gov.sg. Every 2025 constituency adds up to its exact official number of voters.

How accurate are the numbers for each small area?

They are careful estimates. Official figures are only published for whole constituencies, so the simulator spreads each constituency's voters across its neighbourhoods using census and housing data. Totals per 2025 constituency are exact; individual blocks are close approximations.

If I don't change anything, do I get the real 2025 result?

Yes. With the 2025 map, default contests and all swings at zero, the forecast reproduces the real GE2025 result: PAP 87 seats, WP 10. Every contested constituency's vote share is within a fraction of a percent.

Why does Marine Parade–Braddell Heights show a contest?

In 2025 it was a walkover, so nobody actually voted there. For 2030 the simulator assumes the WP will contest it (the WP had planned to in 2025). Its starting vote (about 62% PAP) is an estimate, because there's no real 2025 result to use. To keep it a walkover, remove WP in Step 2.

Are the party profiles and leader roles up to date?

They reflect public information as of September 2026, including the July 2026 Cabinet changes. Profiles are short and deliberately neutral, written for beginners. For each party's full positions, read its own website and manifesto.

How does Auto-draw work? Is it AI?

It's a redistricting algorithm that runs entirely in your web browser, similar to tools used by researchers who study electoral maps. It first places a starting point for each constituency where voters live and grows them outward. It then tries hundreds of thousands of small swaps of neighbourhoods between neighbouring seats, keeping the swaps that make the map fairer, neater or, in gerrymander mode, more one-sided. Nothing is sent to any server.

Why can't I get exactly 97 seats?

You can! Seats = number of SMCs + MPs in each GRC. Adjust GRC sizes (3–6) or add and remove constituencies until the total matches. Parliament can also change size between elections, so any total is allowed. The count is shown as information only.

Why is part of my map pale or white?

Pale areas have no voters: industrial land, the airport, nature reserves and offshore islands. White areas are populated places that aren't in any constituency yet. The Checks box will flag them.

My mouse drags the map instead of painting (or the other way round)

With Paint, Erase or Lasso selected, dragging draws. To move the map instead, hold Space while dragging, switch to the Pan tool (H), or use the scroll wheel to zoom.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes. The map sits on top and the controls below. Drawing boundaries is easier with a mouse on a bigger screen, but forecasting and election night work well on phones.

Keyboard shortcuts

1–4 switch tabs. V Select, B Paint, G Fill, L Lasso, E Erase, H Pan. Space (hold) pans the map. Ctrl+Z undoes and Ctrl+Y redoes.
